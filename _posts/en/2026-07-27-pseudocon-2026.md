On July 25, 2026, I presented at PseudoCon 2026 as part of the AutoBioX team. It was the first time we brought four months of research to a public stage.

PseudoCon is the annual conference organized by PseudoLab (가짜연구소), a Korean AI/ML research community. This year marked the 10th edition, held at Sookmyung Women's University with around 300 attendees. Sessions ran about 35 minutes behind schedule — the audience kept pulling speakers into conversation.

## The Day's Sessions

Different domains, but the sessions converged on the same question: where does AI handle execution, and where does human judgment take over — and how do you design that boundary deliberately. Robotics researchers, a hiring platform founder, a bioinformatics team, agent platform developers — the framing varied, but the underlying tension was the same.

**Jinsoo Shin (KRAFTON)** opened with the idea of "Planned Happenstance" — that career-changing opportunities feel accidental but are the result of consistent, intentional practice. A fitting frame for a room full of people who chose to spend a Saturday learning.

![Jinsoo Shin (KRAFTON) — Opening](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/03_jinsoo_shin.jpg)

**Youngjeon Choi (AWS)** gave the most systematic framing of the day. The shift from stateless (single request-response) to stateful (multi-step, persistent) AI is the defining transition of this period. He drew a clear line between soft guardrails (prompt-level instructions) and hard guardrails (system-level policy enforcement), arguing that agentic systems need both. His summary: AI doesn't replace people — it amplifies the best ones.

![Youngjeon Choi (AWS) — Agentic AI: The Rise of Acting AI](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/04_youngjeon_choi.jpg)

**Jinha Lim (GroupBy HR)** runs a hiring platform and has watched the job market shift in real time. The skill-checklist format is fading. At a hackathon with Musinsa, KakaoPay Securities, and Channel.talk, participants weren't given a problem — they were given data and told to find one. The scarce skill isn't implementation anymore; it's problem definition. Her sharpest line: when AI gets something wrong, it apologizes. When a person makes a bad call that costs the company, someone is accountable. That accountability gap is where human judgment stays irreplaceable.

![Jinha Lim (GroupBy HR) — What Companies Want in the AI Era](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/05_jinha_lim.jpg)

**Yuseek Yang (SK Telecom)** compressed the Physical AI lineage into 15 minutes: from RT1 to VLA to Open VLA, tracing where the bottlenecks moved (language → vision encoder → action speed) and how each generation addressed them. Flow Matching cut the denoising steps from 40 to around 4. He also shared his own Season 12 project results honestly: 36% MSE reduction via Cosmos-based data augmentation, but the robot still can't complete the task in real conditions.

![Yuseek Yang (SK Telecom) — Physical AI: From VLA to WAM](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/06_yuseek_yang.jpg)

**Kenny Lee (RunPod)** gave the day's only English session. RunPod started with two GPUs in a basement; the pitch is 90% cost reduction versus traditional HPC. He walked through three tiers — Pod (virtualized GPU), Cluster (large-scale training), Serverless Endpoint (production deployment) — with a live demo. The company grew through Reddit word-of-mouth without a sales team.

![Kenny Lee (RunPod)](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/07_kenny_lee.jpg)

**Junho Kong (SK On)** walked through recent OpenAI updates in practice. GPT 5.6's Sol, Terra, and Luna aren't a performance ranking — they're different tools for different contexts. He showed Ultra mode's parallel sub-agent execution, Sites (idea to deployment in one flow), and Computer Use, all grounded in concrete use cases. The framing shift he highlighted: from "how much code did Codex write" to "what can I actually ship with Codex."

![Junho Kong (SK On) — OpenAI Codex Developer Update](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/08_junho_kong.jpg)

**Soohyun Kim (HDC Labs)** walked through the stumbles of attaching an A/B testing platform to the PseudoLab service. The key lesson: don't start with the data, start with the question. Early weeks were spent looking for signals before locking down what success would even mean. She also brought real incidents to the stage: a 60-hour data outage in the first days of an experiment, a one-month bottleneck from PR merge to deployment. Her conclusion: an experiment platform alone isn't enough — you need the full loop of deployment, monitoring, and incident recovery.

![Soohyun Kim (HDC Labs) — Making PseudoLab an Experiment-Ready Community](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/09_soohyun_kim.jpg)

Our talk was titled "Multi-Agent Research in the Wild: From Single-Cell to Pathology Images." I presented solo, with a slot of about 15 minutes.

AI-assisted research has well-documented failure modes — a 1.8% reproducibility rate and 40% code failure rate in published benchmarks. AutoBioX tried to address these by structuring the work as a multi-agent team, with separate roles for planning, summarization, prediction, verification, and literature search. Humans stay in the loop for judgment; agents handle execution.

**BioProject01** was a single-cell chromatin multiomics study. Our original target — a chromatin-to-transcription lag metric — came back at Spearman 0.04. Effectively zero. But a transcription velocity measure (α) that emerged through AI-assisted analysis held consistently at 0.82–0.88 across different methods. The signal we weren't targeting turned out to be the more robust one.

**BioProject02** predicted molecular markers (ER/PR/HER2/PAM50) from H&E breast cancer whole-slide images. The clinical picture wasn't uniform: image-based prediction is sufficient for ER and PR, HER2 still needs molecular confirmation, and PAM50 subtypes are useful for prediction but shouldn't drive treatment decisions directly. We stuck to principles and direction in the talk — no numbers until the validation gate closes.

One deliberate choice: we included the failures. GPU hardware issues, a key team member departure, storage I/O bottlenecks — all of it made it onto the slides. PseudoLab is a community where people put failures on stage. AutoBioX followed suit.

**Minjoo Jwa (HF KREW)** automated the Hugging Face English blog pipeline: translation, quality review, SEO, and PR review, all running as an agent loop. When a translation commit lands, the SEO and quality skills run in parallel; their reports become PR comments; the PR agent reads the CI logs and decides whether to revise. GitHub PRs double as the agent's state memory. The concept she introduced — "loop engineering" — is about designing how long and how many cycles to run, not just what to prompt.

![Minjoo Jwa (HF KREW) — Hugging Face Blog Agent](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/11_minjoo_jwa.jpg)

**Chanran Kim (Pseudo Lab)** — speaking as part of the organizing team — closed the main sessions with Betoken: "just as a token predicts what comes next, small contributions and connections can be early signs of growth." Partnerships with NVIDIA, OpenAI, and others traced back to individual contributions that started without expectation of return. PseudoLab itself is the evidence, and so was the work the Season 12 builders had just put on stage.

![Chanran Kim (Pseudo Lab) — Betoken: Signs of Growth](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/12_chanran_kim.jpg)

The Agent Ignite session had several short talks. Here are the ones I caught, in the order I heard them.

**Mina Kim (Worksphere)** built a **work wiki agent** that structures scattered Slack and Notion entries into a résumé draft. She was honest about where it breaks: structuring and cleaning is one thing, but trusting the output is another. Her closing line — "the bottleneck was myself" — landed.

![Agent Ignite: Mina Kim (Worksphere) — Updating your résumé with a work wiki agent](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/15_ignite_kimina.jpg)

**Sangjun Park's Hyam** distributes agents as encrypted packages — the harness stays private, only results are shared. When a demo tried to extract the internal structure, the verification logic refused directly.

![Agent Ignite: Sangjun Park — How far should I share the agent I built?](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/13_ignite_parksangjun.jpg)

**Youngwoong Park (SK Telecom)** built a **Personal LLM Knowledge Base** connecting Obsidian, Confluence, and Slack into a continuously compiled knowledge graph — closer to an always-on wiki than a retrieval system that fetches on demand.

![Agent Ignite: Youngwoong Park (SK Telecom) — Personal Knowledge Bases](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/14_ignite_parkyoungwoong.jpg)

PseudoLab is a voluntary learning community of AI/ML researchers, developers, and practitioners who form project teams each season. Alongside the talk sessions, Season 12 teams ran project booths. The experiment platform team I was part of staffed theirs for four hours — I was in presentation prep mode and couldn't make it over, which I regretted.

![Season 12 booth — Cosmos × GROOT robot foundation model](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/16_booth_poster.jpg)

One corner of the venue had a board with the question: "What's the one real thing you want to take away from PseudoLab?" Post-its in every handwriting, each a different answer. That board probably captured the community better than any slide.

!["What's the one real thing you want to take away from PseudoLab?" — audience board](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/17_board.jpg)

## After the Conference

The afterparty gave me a chance to finally meet people I'd only known online. Finding real common ground with people from very different backgrounds was the kind of thing that only happens in person. I didn't get enough time with the organizers who made the whole day possible — that's the one thing I'd do differently.

![Afterparty venue](/assets/images/pseudocon-2026/18_afterparty.jpg)

## What's Next

Four months of work, first public showing. The papers aren't done — numbers stay private until the validation gate closes, which is why the talk stuck to principles and direction. That's not a limitation; it's the standard we set for ourselves.

Next PseudoCon, I'm bringing a paper.
