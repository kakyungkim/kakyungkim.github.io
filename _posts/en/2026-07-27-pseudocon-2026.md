On July 25, 2026, I presented at PseudoCon 2026 as part of the AutoBioX team. It was the first time we brought six months of research to a public stage.

PseudoCon is the annual conference organized by PseudoLab (가짜연구소), a Korean AI/ML research community. This year marked the 10th edition, held at Sookmyung Women's University with around 300 attendees. The day opened with a small coincidence: as I finished the morning rehearsal, Anthropic announced Claude Opus 5. One of the speakers mentioned it mid-session while flipping through slides, and the room laughed. An AI community conference, and the field had already moved forward.

## What We Presented

Our talk was titled "Multi-Agent Research in the Wild: From Single-Cell to Pathology Images." I co-presented with Sangjun Park, our slot running about 14 minutes.

The setup: AI-assisted research has well-documented failure modes — a 1.8% reproducibility rate and 40% code failure rate. AutoBioX tried to address these by structuring work as a multi-agent team, with separate roles for planning, summarization, prediction, verification, and literature search. Humans stay in the loop for judgment; agents handle execution.

We shared results from two projects.

**BioProject01** was a single-cell chromatin multiomics study. Our original target — a chromatin-to-transcription lag metric — came back at Spearman 0.04. Effectively zero. But during analysis, a transcription velocity measure (α) that emerged through AI-assisted exploration held consistently at 0.82–0.88 across different methods. The metric we weren't targeting turned out to be the more robust signal.

**BioProject02** predicted molecular markers (ER/PR/HER2/PAM50) from H&E breast cancer whole-slide images. The clinical takeaway wasn't uniform: image-based prediction works well enough for ER and PR, HER2 still needs molecular confirmation, and PAM50 subtypes are best suited for prediction rather than treatment decisions. We stuck to principles and directions in the talk — no numbers until the validation gate closes.

One deliberate choice: we included the failures. GPU hardware issues, a key team member departure, storage I/O bottlenecks — all of it made it onto the slides.

## What Ran Through the Day

One phrase repeated across sessions: "AI executes, humans judge." Robotics researchers, a hiring platform founder, bioinformatics people, agent platform developers — different contexts, same landing point.

The community normalized sharing failure. A 36% MSE improvement on paper, but the robot still can't perform the task. A 60-hour data outage mid-experiment. These weren't buried in a limitations section — they were the main story.

Joomin Jwa's session on a Hugging Face blog automation agent illustrated the same principle from a different angle: the key to building agent systems isn't prompt crafting, it's designing loops that detect errors and self-correct, with humans reviewing only the final output. The setup connected GitHub Actions, PR comments, and CI logs so the agent iterated on its own work.

## After the Conference

The afterparty gave me a chance to finally meet people I'd only known online. Finding real common ground with people from very different backgrounds was the kind of thing that only happens in person. I didn't get enough time with the organizers who made the whole day possible — that's the one thing I'd do differently.

## What's Next

Both projects had their first public showing. The papers aren't done. Numbers stay private until verification is complete.

Next PseudoCon, I'm bringing a paper.
